What a session looks like

Every workshop moves from curiosity to evidence to action. No lectures, no worksheets to take home.

Investigate

Teams open a case file and hunt for the first clue: who got sick, when, and what they had in common.

Experiment

Glitter germs, glow powder, and petri prints show how something invisible travels across a classroom.

Solve

Each team presents its conclusion and one change the school could make. Every case ends with a plan.

A 45-minute case, minute by minute

  1. Warm-up: spot the germ

    A quick round of true-or-false gets everyone talking about how illness actually spreads.

  2. The outbreak drops

    Students meet the case and the first three patients. Questions go on the board.

  3. Hands-on test

    An experiment gives teams real data to work with, not a page of numbers we handed them.

  4. Find patient zero

    Teams map contacts, compare timelines, and argue their case with evidence.

  5. Pitch the fix

    Two minutes per team: what happened, and what would stop it next time.

About the program

Public health makes sense when you get to solve something.

Disease Detectives teaches elementary and middle schoolers the basics of epidemiology by handing students a mystery instead of a textbook. Kids learn what germs are, how outbreaks begin, and how scientists figure out where an illness started — then practice the same thinking on cases built for their age.

Our mission

Health information reaches kids as a list of rules: wash your hands, cover your cough, stay home when you are sick. Rules are easy to forget. Reasoning sticks.

We want every student to leave a session able to ask a scientist's question — who got sick, when, and what did they share — and to feel like public health is something they could do one day.

What students learn

  • How germs spread through air, touch, food, and water
  • What an outbreak is and how it grows
  • Reading a timeline and spotting a pattern in data
  • Contact tracing and the idea of patient zero
  • Prevention that actually works, and why it works
  • Explaining a conclusion so other people believe it

How we teach

Hands before heads

Students touch the experiment first and get the vocabulary after, once they have something to attach it to.

Kid-safe science

Every activity uses harmless materials — glow lotion, food coloring, washable powder. Nothing infectious, ever.

Youth-led

Sessions are designed and run by students not far ahead of the ones in the room, with adult supervision throughout.

Signature experiments

Glow-powder handshake

Harmless lotion plus a UV light reveals exactly where "clean" hands still carry something.

Sneeze range

A spray bottle and a paper runway measure how far droplets travel with and without a covered cough.

Petri prints

Students press fingertips into agar plates before and after washing, then check results the following week.

Cup exchange

Colored water traded around the room shows how quickly one source reaches almost everyone.

Filter the well

Sand, gravel, and cloth turn cloudy water clear — and start a conversation about waterborne illness.

Teams plot their own case counts by day and find the spike without being told where it is.

Curriculum

A 12-month program, one badge at a time.

For our ongoing club, Disease Detectives runs as a 12-month curriculum — one session a month. Every session follows the same trusted shape, so kids always know what's coming — and always leave with something new toward their next badge.

How every session runs

45–60 minutes, the same five beats every session, so kids feel safe, engaged, and excited.

  1. Warm-up mystery prompt

    A clue, a symptom, or a strange scenario kicks things off and gets the room guessing.

  2. Mini-lesson

    5–10 minutes of simple science — just enough to make sense of what's coming next.

  3. Hands-on activity

    An experiment, craft, or simulation puts that idea into the kids' own hands.

  4. Detective debrief

    Kids explain what they discovered, out loud, in their own words.

  5. Badge progress

    Points go toward a skill badge — a sticker or stamp to mark real progress.

Skill badges

Each badge represents a real scientific skill, earned through the week's activity rather than handed out for showing up.

Observation

Noticing clues that matter.

Logic

Connecting evidence into a conclusion.

Communication

Explaining findings so others follow.

Lab safety

Following procedure, every time.

Teamwork

Collaborating to crack the case.

Data detective

Reading a chart and spotting the pattern.

More badges can be added as the program grows.

Curriculum units

Months 1–3

Unit 1 · Germ basics

What germs are, the difference between bacteria, viruses, and fungi, and how they travel through air, surfaces, and hands.

Months 4–7

Unit 2 · Outbreak investigation

Kids learn to think like epidemiologists: finding case zero, mapping transmission, analyzing symptoms, and tracing contacts.

Months 8–10

Unit 3 · Healthy habits & prevention

The science of handwashing, immune system basics, nutrition, sleep, and an age-appropriate look at vaccines.

Months 11–12

Unit 4 · Final project & showcase

Kids choose a project — solve a new outbreak, build a health poster, or give a mini presentation — then present it on Showcase Day.

Extended programs can add an optional Data Detectives unit — reading charts, spotting patterns, and making predictions from simple data sets.

Outbreak simulations

A mystery with real evidence, solved by the room.

A simulation is the heart of the program. Students receive a fictional outbreak built on real epidemiology, then work as a team of investigators: gather clues, track the first case, look at the numbers, and recommend what to do next.

Data

Case counts by day, symptom lists, and a seating chart. Students plot the numbers themselves and find the spike.

Clues

Interview cards, a kitchen log, a field trip permission slip. Some clues matter, some are there to test their judgment.

Teamwork

Each detective holds a piece nobody else has, so the case only comes together when the team talks it through.

How students crack a case

  1. Read the briefing

    Who is sick, what are the symptoms, and when did each person start feeling ill?

  2. Build the timeline

    Cases go on a chart by day. The shape of that chart is the first real piece of evidence.

  3. Find what they shared

    Teams compare what each patient ate, touched, or attended, and cross off what does not fit.

  4. Trace back to patient zero

    Contact links get drawn on a map until the earliest case stands out.

  5. Test the theory

    An experiment either supports the team's explanation or sends them back to the clues. Both count as science.

  6. Recommend a fix

    Teams propose one realistic change and explain who it protects.

Frequently asked questions

Is there a cost to attend a workshop?

Open community sessions are free. School and organization bookings are scheduled directly with us — reach out through this form and we'll confirm details for your group.

Is the science safe for kids?

Yes. Every activity uses harmless, washable materials — things like glow lotion, food coloring, and craft supplies. Nothing infectious or hazardous is ever used, and an adult leader is present at every session.

What age range is this really for?

Sessions are designed for elementary and middle schoolers. Activities are adjusted by age, so a kindergarten class and a 7th grade classroom get different versions of the same case.

How many kids can a workshop hold?

A classroom session works best for 15–35 students. Assembly-style formats can accommodate up to about 120. Let us know your group size and we'll recommend a format.

What do you need from our school or venue?

A room with tables or desks, access to a sink, and a staff member present. We bring every material, handout, and piece of equipment.

Can my child volunteer if they're under 18?

Yes — many of our session leaders are middle and high schoolers. Volunteers under 18 complete a short training alongside a parent or guardian sign-off before leading any activity.

How far do you travel for bookings?

We currently serve the greater Memphis area, including Germantown and Collierville. If you're outside that range, ask anyway — we're happy to discuss options.
